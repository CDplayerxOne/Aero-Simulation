Solving the flow

Both tunnels begin with a uniform stream of air. A grid divides the test section into small volumes called voxels. A solid face blocks air moving through it and allows air to slide along it. The solver finds the change in velocity needed to divert the stream around those obstacles.

For steady, incompressible, irrotational flow, the velocity disturbance is the gradient of a scalar potential, φ. Conservation of mass gives ∇²φ = 0 in the fluid. The code repeatedly updates each fluid cell using its open neighbours and the no-through-flow condition on blocked faces. Adding the resulting gradient to the incoming velocity gives a three-dimensional velocity field, expressed relative to the inlet speed.

Car demo
A 111 × 25 × 53 grid with 0.105 m spacing represents the chassis, wheels, wings and floor above a slip ground plane. Yaw turns the incoming flow sideways. Rear wing incidence changes the wing obstacle. The solve allows up to 150 relaxation sweeps.
Mesh playground
A 72 × 41 × 43 grid with 0.12 m spacing represents the fitted mesh in a stream along +X. The worker projects every triangle onto three grid planes to mark the surface, then fills pairs of intersections along X for closed volumes. It rotates the mesh to the chosen orientation and angle of attack before voxelizing it. The solve allows up to 125 sweeps.

What a streamline shows. Starting at an inlet seed, the code samples velocity, takes a half-step, then uses the midpoint velocity for the next step. Each line traces a direction through one steady solution. Moving highlights show travel along it. Pausing freezes the animation. In the demo, prescribed wheel wakes, wing-tip swirl and underfloor flow modify what the tracers follow.

From speed to pressure

Let U be inlet air speed in m/s and u the local speed. Both tunnels use air density ρ = 1.225 kg/m³ and dynamic viscosity μ = 1.81 × 10−5 Pa·s. Three quantities connect the flow picture to the instruments.

Dynamic pressure q = ½ρU²
The pressure scale for aerodynamic loads, measured in pascals (N/m²). Doubling inlet speed quadruples q. The local static pressure can be higher or lower than the inlet static pressure.
Pressure coefficient Cp = (p − p∞) / q
Bernoulli’s relation gives Cp = 1 − (u/U)² in the base model. Air at half the inlet speed gives +0.75. Air at 1.5 times inlet speed gives −1.25. A negative value means suction relative to the inlet.
Reynolds number Re = ρUL / μ
This dimensionless ratio compares inertial and viscous effects at a chosen length L. Both dashboards display it in millions. Viscosity enters this readout only. Changing speed scales Re without triggering transition or turbulence in the solver.

Car demo telemetry

The demo estimates whole-car coefficients from rear wing incidence α and yaw β. Its fixed reference length is 5.50 m and its reference area is 1.50 m². The following curves define the car shown here. Angles below are written in degrees.

w = 0.95 sin(α + 4°) / sin(22°)
CL = −(2.17 + w) cos2.3(β)
CD = 0.765 + 0.145w² + 0.95 sin²(β)

The dimensionless term w represents wing loading. Increasing incidence increases its contribution to downforce and adds a quadratic drag penalty. Yaw reduces the vertical load and adds drag symmetrically to either side. The dashboard reports drag = qACD along the wind axis and downforce = −qACL. It divides newtons by 1,000 for the kN readouts. A downward load has a negative lift coefficient.

What makes the car’s pressure colours?

The shader samples speed just outside the body and converts it to Cp. It raises pressure on wind-facing surfaces and adds prescribed loading on the wings and floor. Under the car, a separate passage model speeds up the air as the roof height shrinks, then lets it slow through the diffuser. The amber corkscrews follow a prescribed counter-rotating vortex pair. These approximations explain the visual patterns. The dashboard takes its loads directly from the coefficient curves above.

Establish the scale

An upload is centred and scaled to fit the test section, with room for rotation. The dimensions under telemetry are in fitted tunnel metres. After applying the X, Y and Z orientation controls, the solver measures the full mesh at zero angle of attack. Its streamwise extent is the reference length L for Re. If the bounding-box spans are x, y and z, the reference area is A = max(xy, yz, zx). Changing mesh orientation can change both references. Sweeping angle of attack holds them fixed.

Sum the pressure on exposed faces

For each fluid cell touching a solid voxel, the solver computes Cp from its velocity. Each shared face covers ΔA = (0.12 m)². With n the outward unit normal of the solid face, its contribution is ΔF = −q Cp n ΔA. Positive pressure pushes inward. Suction pulls outward. Summing the X components gives signed pressure drag along the stream. Summing the Y components gives lift, positive upward. Both readouts use newtons.

One face at 35 m/s. Here q ≈ 750.3 Pa. An upstream-facing patch with Cp = +0.75 pushes downstream by 750.3 × 0.75 × 0.0144 ≈ 8.1 N. Other faces can reinforce or cancel this contribution.

Pressure drag and lift coefficients
Divide the summed drag or lift by qA to get CD,p or CL. Holding the mesh pose fixed and doubling U quadruples both forces while the coefficients stay constant.
Minimum and maximum surface Cp
These are the lowest and highest values in fluid cells adjacent to the voxel surface. The heatmap stores a colour range from −2 to +1 and interpolates near the displayed surface. The force sum and extrema use the original, unclipped values, so a minimum below −2 can share the same colour as −2.
Peak surface airspeed
The smallest adjacent-cell pressure corresponds to the fastest sampled flow. Inverting Bernoulli gives upeak = U √(1 − Cp,min), in m/s. An unresolved surface leaves the load and extrema readouts blank.

Reading the limits

Small drag can be a feature of the mathematics
In ideal potential flow, pressures on the front and rear of a closed body can balance. Real skin friction and a separated wake contribute drag that the playground omits. Voxel asymmetry and finite resolution can leave a small residual of either sign. A slightly negative result gives little evidence of real thrust.
A wing can stall in the real tunnel
The playground has no circulation condition at a sharp trailing edge and no boundary layer that can detach. It can miss much of a wing’s real lift and cannot locate its stall angle. The demo’s illustrative lift curve and added wake patterns likewise give no calibrated stall or vortex-strength prediction.
A smooth preview can conceal coarse physics
The full uploaded triangle list feeds the same fixed grid even when the display uses fewer triangles. A narrow slot can close, a thin fin can disappear, and a small rotation can move a surface into a different row of cells. Open or overlapping shells can also make the interior ambiguous. More triangles alone do not refine the flow grid.
Scale and time matter
The fitted playground geometry determines force and Reynolds number. Two exports of the same shape in different source units can produce the same result. Both tunnels assume constant density and a steady inlet. Gust response, transient shedding, compressibility and a rotating tyre’s viscous interaction with the road lie outside these models.

For a useful comparison, hold the mesh orientation and speed fixed and change angle of attack alone. Watch how the pressure pattern moves as well as how the integrated lift changes. For absolute vehicle loads, compare against measurements or a validated viscous-flow solution at the same physical scale and Reynolds number.
